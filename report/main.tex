% ================================================================ 1 INTRODUCTION
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% SCOPE: what the Upsilon states are and why their known masses make them a test of the
%%        analysis; the physics the method and results refer back to (narrow widths -> detector
%%        peak shape; resolution delta m / m ~ delta p / p; radiative tail; momentum scale
%%        delta m = alpha m, 3e-4); aim; roadmap. ~1 page.
%% REFS:  ParticleDataGroup:2024cfk; LHCb:2013itw; Skwarnicki:1986xj; LHCb:2023ood.

Particle accelerators and detectors are fundamental in the process of discovery in high-energy physics, where theoretical predictions of the Standard Model are tested against experimental data.
Accepting or rejecting hypotheses from theory, however, requires substantial precision in the measurement of particle states, leading to the need for reliable calibration targets for particle detectors to reach such precision.
$\Upsilon$ mesons are bound states of a bottom quark and its antiquark ($b\bar b$), whose three lowest states, \Ups{1S}, \Ups{2S} and \Ups{3S}, appear as narrow peaks in the dimuon
mass spectrum between 9 and 11 \GeVcc, and their masses are known to better than 0.5 \MeVcc ~\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2024cfk}. 

The \Ups{1S}, \Ups{2S} and \Ups{3S} are the $n = 1, 2, 3$ radial states with the quark
spins aligned and no orbital angular momentum ($n\,{}^3S_1$). This gives them
$J^{PC} = 1^{--}$, the quantum numbers of the photon, so the $b\bar b$ pair can annihilate
through a virtual photon into a lepton pair. Decays into a $B\bar B$ pair are forbidden by
energy conservation below threshold (about \SI{10.56}{GeV/\clight^2}). The remaining strong
decays must go through three gluons, which is strongly suppressed (the OZI rule). As a
result the natural widths are only about 54, 32 and \SI{20}{keV}\todo{PDG 2024}, and the
dimuon branching fractions are 2--2.5\%\todo{PDG 2024}. At the LHC the $\Upsilon$ states
are produced mainly through gluon--gluon fusion. LHCb, a forward spectrometer covering
$2 < \eta < 5$, records them in large numbers~\cite{LHCb:2013itw}.

With natural widths of tens of keV, the observed width of each peak, tens of MeV, comes
almost entirely from the detector. The dimuon mass is computed from the two measured muon
momenta and their opening angle. At these momenta the momentum term dominates, so
$\delta m/m \approx \delta p/p$: the three peaks share the same relative resolution, and
their absolute widths grow with mass. The momentum itself is measured from how much each
track bends in the magnet. A faster track bends less, so its momentum is measured less
precisely, and the resolution varies with the muons' momenta and directions.
Photons radiated by the muons lower the reconstructed mass of some candidates, which gives each
peak a tail on its low-mass side, conventionally described by a Crystal Ball
function~\cite{Skwarnicki:1986xj}. Finally, a miscalibration of the momentum scale,
$p \to (1+\alpha)p$, shifts every mass by $\delta m = \alpha m$. LHCb calibrates this scale to
$3\times10^{-4}$~\cite{LHCb:2023ood}, about \SI{3}{MeV/\clight^2} at the $\Upsilon$ masses.

\Cref{sec:method} describes the data, the fitting method and the mass models.
\Cref{sec:results} follows the project tasks: Gaussian fits to one and then three peaks,
extended signal models, kinematic selection and binning, and finally the systematic
uncertainties and the measured masses. \Cref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
