\begin{abstract}
%% SCOPE: 50–100 words. The aim (absolute Upsilon masses from extended maximum-likelihood fits),
%%        the method in one sentence (double Crystal Ball, selection, kinematic bins, systematics),
%%        the result (three masses with stat / fit-model / momentum-scale uncertainties) and the
%%        conclusion (momentum scale dominates; fit-model uncertainty below 1 MeV). Written LAST.

The masses of the \Ups{1S}, \Ups{2S} and \Ups{3S} mesons are measured with extended binned maximum-likelihood fits to dimuon data recorded by LHCb in 2015 at $\sqrt{s} = 13$\,TeV.
A double Crystal Ball signal model is fitted simultaneously in 12 cells of rapidity and transverse momentum, after a selection on the soft muon \pT{} and a correction for the shape bias measured on simulation.
The masses are $9455.95 \pm 0.13 \pm 0.19 \pm 2.84$, $10018.44 \pm 0.27 \pm 0.25 \pm 3.01$ and $10349.86 \pm 0.44 \pm 0.71 \pm 3.10\,\MeVcc{}$, where the uncertainties are statistical, fit model and momentum scale.
All three lie about 1.6 standard deviations below their PDG values, consistent with a single momentum-scale offset of $(-4.75 \pm 0.14)\times10^{-4}$, which dominates the uncertainty.

\end{abstract}

% ================================================================ 1 INTRODUCTION
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% SCOPE: what the Upsilon states are and why their known masses make them a test of the
%%        analysis; the physics the method and results refer back to (narrow widths -> detector
%%        peak shape; resolution delta m / m ~ delta p / p; radiative tail; momentum scale
%%        delta m = alpha m, 3e-4); aim; roadmap. ~1 page.
%% REFS:  ParticleDataGroup:2024cfk; LHCb:2013itw; Skwarnicki:1986xj; LHCb:2023ood.

Particle accelerators and detectors are fundamental in the process of discovery in high-energy physics, where theoretical predictions of the Standard Model are tested against experimental data.
Accepting or rejecting hypotheses from theory, however, requires precise measurements of particle states, and so detectors need reliable calibration targets.
The ideal target is a particle whose mass is known far better than the detector can measure it, and whose natural width is negligible, so that its peak in the data reflects the detector alone.
The $\Upsilon$ mesons, bound states of a bottom quark and its antiquark ($b\bar b$), come close to this ideal.
Their three lowest states, \Ups{1S}, \Ups{2S} and \Ups{3S}, decay to muon pairs and appear as narrow peaks in the dimuon mass spectrum between 9 and 11\,\GeVcc{}, with masses known to better than 0.5\,\MeVcc{}~\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2024cfk}.
Lying below the threshold for decay into a pair of $B$ mesons, they can decay only through the strongly suppressed annihilation of the $b\bar b$ pair, which gives natural widths of only 54, 32 and \SI{20}{keV}~\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2024cfk}.

The observed peaks, tens of MeV wide, are therefore almost entirely detector resolution.
The mass resolution is dominated by the momentum resolution, so $\delta m/m \approx \delta p/p$ and the three peaks share the same relative width.
Since the momentum is measured from the curvature of each track in the magnetic field, the resolution worsens for stiffer tracks and varies with the muon kinematics.
Photons radiated by the muons lower the reconstructed mass of some candidates and give each peak a low-mass tail, conventionally described by a Crystal Ball function.
The peak positions, in turn, test the momentum calibration, as a scale error $p \to (1+\alpha)p$ shifts every mass by $\delta m = \alpha m$.
LHCb calibrates this scale to $3\times10^{-4}$~\cite{LHCb:2023ood}, or about 3\,\MeVcc{} at the $\Upsilon$ masses.

This report measures the three $\Upsilon$ masses with extended maximum-likelihood fits to dimuon candidates recorded in 2015 by LHCb, a forward spectrometer designed for the study of particles containing $b$ or $c$ quarks~\cite{LHCb:2013itw}.
Since the true masses are known, the measurement also tests the analysis itself, including the peak-shape model, the dependence on the selection and muon kinematics, and the estimate of the uncertainties.
\Cref{sec:method} describes the data, the fitting method and the mass models.
\Cref{sec:results} follows the project tasks, from Gaussian fits to one and three peaks, through extended signal models and the kinematic selection and binning, to the systematic uncertainties and the final masses.
\Cref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Data and simulation}
\label{sec:m-data}
%% SCOPE: full 2015 sample (number of candidates, 9-11 GeV/c^2) and its six observables; the
%%        simulated Upsilon(1S) sample and its generated mass (PDG 2016); the soft muon (the
%%        muon of the pair with the lower pT), the selection min(pT1, pT2) >= 3 GeV/c motivated
%%        in Sec. 3.3; the fiducial region 2 < y < 4.5, pT < 15 GeV/c for the kinematic cells.
%%        Tasks 1-4 use all events, everything from the signal-shape study on the selected ones.
%% NUMBERS: 01_measure_masses.py (event counts); 03_selection.py.
%% REFS:  ParticleDataGroup:2016lqr.

The data consist of 4\,689\,467 dimuon candidates recorded by LHCb in 2015 in proton--proton collisions at $\sqrt{s} = \SI{13}{TeV}$, with masses between 9.0 and 11.0\,\GeVcc{} (\cref{fig:variables}).
Each candidate records the dimuon mass $m$, transverse momentum \pT{}, pseudorapidity $\eta$ and momentum $p$, and the transverse momenta $p_{\mathrm{T},1}$ and $p_{\mathrm{T},2}$ of the two muons.
Rapidity $y$ is then calculated from the candidate particle $m$, $p$, and \pT{}, for usage described later in this section.
Most candidates are combinatorial background from pairs of unrelated muons or misidentified hadrons~\cite{LHCb:2013itw}.

A sample of 398\,842 simulated \Ups{1S} decays, with no background, accompanies the data.
The sample carries no record of its generator settings.
As it accompanies the 2015 data, we assume it was generated with the world-average mass of that time, $m_{1S} = 9460.30\,\MeVcc$~\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2016lqr}, and use it to measure how far the fitted peak position lies from the true mass for each signal model (\cref{sec:r-shape}).

The background is reduced by requiring the soft muon, the muon of the pair with the lower transverse momentum, to have $\ptmin \geq 3\,\GeVc$.
Background candidates contain more low-momentum muons than \Ups{nS} decays, so the cut removes background faster than signal (\cref{sec:r-selection}).
The cut is applied to both samples and keeps 1\,862\,718 candidates in data and 302\,160 in simulation.
The Gaussian fits of \cref{sec:r-gauss} use all candidates, and all later fits use the selected ones.
The final fit in kinematic cells is further restricted to the fiducial region of the earlier LHCb measurement~\cite{LHCb:2013itw}, $2.0 < y < 4.5$ and $\pT < 15\,\GeVc$, which contains 1\,803\,404 selected candidates.

\subsection{Fitting and statistics}
\label{sec:m-fits}
%% SCOPE: extended binned likelihood (250 bins; yields as parameters); components normalised on
%%        the fit window; MIGRAD minimisation, errors from HESSE, MINOS and -2 ln L scans
%%        (profiled vs other parameters fixed); safeguards against the double-CB false minimum
%%        (start from a reference fit). Goodness of fit: Baker-Cousins chi2, runs test on the
%%        residual signs; likelihood ratio (Wilks) for nested models, AIC/BIC otherwise;
%%        pseudo-experiments for bias and error coverage (summary in Appendix).
%% REFS:  Cowan:2024stat; wiki:runs; wiki:AIC; wiki:BIC.

All fits to data are extended binned maximum-likelihood fits with 250 bins across the fit window.
The expected count in bin $i$ is
\begin{equation}
    \nu_i = \sum_k N_k \int_{\mathrm{bin}\,i} f_k(m)\,\mathrm{d}m ,
\end{equation}
where $N_k$ is the yield of component $k$ (the three peaks and the background) and $f_k$ its shape, normalised on the fit window.
Floating the yields lets their errors include the Poisson fluctuation of the total number of events.
The fit minimises
\begin{equation}
    -2\ln L = 2\sum_i \left[\nu_i - n_i + n_i \ln\frac{n_i}{\nu_i}\right],
    \label{eq:nll}
\end{equation}
which equals the Poisson $-2\ln L$ for the observed counts $n_i$ up to a constant.
The bins are much narrower than the peaks ($\sigma_{1S} \approx 43\,\MeVcc$), so binning loses almost no information (\cref{tab:bins}).
The background-free simulation is fitted unbinned, since binned double Crystal Ball fits to it do not always converge.

The likelihood is minimised with MIGRAD from MINUIT~\cite{James:1975dr}, through iminuit~\cite{dembinski2026iminuit}.
Models with more parameters are less tightly constrained by the data, and their parameters can trade off against one another, so the minimiser may settle in a false minimum that it still reports as converged.
We therefore accept a fit as valid only if MINUIT reports a converged minimum with an accurate covariance matrix and no parameter at a limit.
A fit that fails these checks is restarted, after a SIMPLEX pass if needed.
The simultaneous fit in kinematic cells starts from the result of a fit to all cells combined.

Parameter errors are taken from HESSE.
For the \Ups{1S} fit they are compared with MINOS and with scans of $-2\ln L$, where the 68\% interval is the range over which $-2\ln L$ stays within one unit of its minimum.
The scans are made with the other parameters either re-minimised at each point (the profile likelihood) or fixed at their best-fit values~\cite{Cowan:2024stat}.

Fit quality is judged by two complementary tests.
The value of \cref{eq:nll} at the minimum is the Baker--Cousins $\chi^2$~\cite[Eq.~(40.16)]{Cowan:2024stat}, which for large expected counts follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with $250 - k$ degrees of freedom for $k$ free parameters.
The $\chi^2$ ignores the order of the bins, so the signs of the residuals $n_i - \nu_i$ are also tested for runs~\cite{wiki:runs}.
A model that fails in one region leaves long stretches of residuals with the same sign.
Nested models are compared with the likelihood ratio, whose change in $-2\ln L$ follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with as many degrees of freedom as extra parameters~\cite{Cowan:2024stat}.
Non-nested models are ranked by the Akaike and Bayesian information criteria~\cite{wiki:AIC,wiki:BIC}, $\mathrm{AIC} = -2\ln L + 2k$ and $\mathrm{BIC} = -2\ln L + k\ln N$, with $N$ the number of candidates.
Finally, 500 pseudo-experiments generated from the baseline fit and refitted in the same way test the fitted masses for bias and their errors for coverage (\cref{tab:toys}).

\subsection{Mass models}
\label{sec:m-models}
%% SCOPE: Gaussian (brief), double Gaussian, Crystal Ball and double Crystal Ball (baseline)
%%        signal; exponential background. The two constraints that make the three-peak fit
%%        identifiable: tails shared by all peaks; widths tied, sigma_k = sigma_1S m_k / m_1S.
%%        Simultaneous fit in 12 (y, pT) cells: masses and tails common, sigma_1S, yields and
%%        background slope per cell.
%% REFS:  Skwarnicki:1986xj; LHCb:2013itw.

The simplest signal model is a Gaussian, which would describe the peak if every candidate had the same resolution.
Two effects break this.
Radiated photons give each peak a low-mass tail, and the resolution varies with the muon kinematics, which makes the inclusive peak a blend of Gaussians of different widths.
The Crystal Ball function addresses the first effect by joining a Gaussian core to a power-law tail at $\alpha$ standard deviations below the mean,
\begin{equation}
    f_\mathrm{CB}(m) \propto
    \begin{cases}
        \exp\!\left(-t^2/2\right), & t > -\alpha, \\[2pt]
        \left(\dfrac{n}{\alpha}\right)^{\!n} e^{-\alpha^2/2} \left(\dfrac{n}{\alpha} - \alpha - t\right)^{\!-n}, & t \leq -\alpha,
    \end{cases}
    \qquad t = \frac{m - \mu}{\sigma},
\end{equation}
where $n$ sets how slowly the tail falls and the two pieces join smoothly at $t = -\alpha$.
The baseline model, the double Crystal Ball, also addresses the second effect.
It is the sum of two Crystal Balls with a common mean and tail, widths $\sigma$ and $r\sigma$ ($r > 1$), and a fraction $f$ in the narrower component.
A double Gaussian, the same construction without the tail, is also tested.
The background is an exponential, $e^{-c\,m}$, with a free slope $c$.

The three-peak fit needs two constraints, because the \Ups{2S} and \Ups{3S} peaks are smaller, overlap, and sit on more background.
The tail parameters ($\alpha$, $n$, and for the double Crystal Ball $r$ and $f$) are shared by all three peaks, since the radiation and the spread of relative resolutions are common to all three.
The widths are tied to the \Ups{1S} width by the mass ratio, $\sigma_k = \sigma_{1S}\, m_k/m_{1S}$ with $m_k$ the PDG masses, following $\delta m/m \approx \delta p/p$.
The fit then has three masses, one width, one tail shape, three signal yields, and the background yield and slope.

Since the resolution depends on the kinematics, the final fit is a simultaneous fit in 12 cells of the fiducial region, three in rapidity (edges 2.0, 3.0, 3.5, 4.5) by four in \pT{} (edges 0, 3, 6, 10, 15\,\GeVc).
The masses and the tail shape are common to all cells, while each cell has its own width, yields and background slope.
The total $-2\ln L$ is the sum over the cells.

The fit gives $\mu_{1S} = 9455.90 \pm 0.10\,\MeVcc$ and $\sigma_{1S} = 43.25 \pm 0.10\,\MeVcc$ (\cref{tab:gauss-params}).

The pulls show where the Gaussian fails.
Data exceed the fit by more than five standard deviations on the low-mass side of the peak, where radiated photons move candidates, and the core is sharper than a Gaussian with heavier wings on both sides.

\subsection{Extended signal models}
\label{sec:r-shape}
%% SCOPE: brief task 5. Simulation (pure signal, known mass) shows the core and the radiative
%%        tail; simulation tails rejected by data (likelihood ratio 586 for 4); model comparison
%%        (Gaussian -> double CB); why the double CB is the baseline despite p(chi2) ~ 2e-4.

%% NUMBERS: 02_signal_shape.py --binned (signal_shape.json).

Task 5 replaces the Gaussian with the shapes of \cref{sec:m-models}.
Each is first fitted to the simulation, where the mass is known and there is no background, so the fit sees the detector response alone.
The double Crystal Ball describes the simulation best, lowering $-2\ln L$ by 8293 relative to the Crystal Ball and by 17\,294 relative to the double Gaussian, each for two extra parameters.
Its simulated shape is a narrow core holding 91\% of the peak, a second component twice as wide, and a tail starting 1.9 standard deviations below the mean (\cref{tab:shape}).

The shapes are then fitted to the three peaks in the selected data, with the tails shared and the widths tied (\cref{tab:comparison}).
Each extension improves on the Gaussian, and the double Crystal Ball improves the most.
Its $\chi^2/\mathrm{ndof}$ falls to 1.36 from 12.85 for the Gaussian, and AIC and BIC prefer it over every other model by more than 500.
The structure the Gaussian leaves at each peak largely disappears from its pulls (\cref{fig:fit-3peaks}).

In data the narrow component holds only 62\% of the peak, and the tail starts closer to the core ($\alpha = 1.52$ against 1.93) but falls faster ($n = 3.8$ against 1.1).
This suggests that the simulation does not reproduce the full spread of resolutions in data, and on data the tail also competes with the background beneath it.
The tails are therefore left free in all later fits.

The double Crystal Ball is still not a perfect description, with $p(\chi^2) = 2\times10^{-4}$ and $p(\mathrm{runs}) = 10^{-3}$.
With 1.9 million candidates, small differences in shape become significant.
It remains the baseline as the best of the tested models on every criterion, and the shift from replacing it with a single Crystal Ball enters the systematic uncertainty (\cref{sec:r-final}).

The shape matters for the mass.
Across the five models of \cref{tab:comparison}, $\mu_{1S}$ moves by up to 1.25\,\MeVcc{}, more than ten times its statistical error (\cref{app:shape}).
Part of this is a bias of the shape itself, which the simulation measures directly.
The double Crystal Ball fitted to simulation returns a peak $0.78 \pm 0.08\,\MeVcc$ above the generated mass, and \cref{sec:r-final} subtracts this bias.
The extra shape parameters are strongly correlated with one another.
The narrow fraction trades off against each width and the width ratio, and $\alpha$ against $n$, since each pair describes the same feature of the peak in two ways (\cref{tab:shape}).
No mass is correlated above 0.5 with any parameter, so this freedom does not reach the masses, and pseudo-experiments find them unbiased with correctly estimated errors (\cref{app:toys}).

\subsection{Systematic uncertainties and final masses}
\label{sec:r-final}

The baseline measurement is the simultaneous double Crystal Ball fit in cells, with each mass corrected for the shape bias found on simulation.
The bias is scaled to each peak by the ratio of its width to the simulated width before it is subtracted.

The fit-model uncertainty comes from four variations, each a refit with one change from the baseline.
These are a single Crystal Ball signal, soft muon cuts at 2.5 and 3.5\,\GeVc{}, and an inclusive fit without cells.
Each variation is corrected with its own simulated bias, so the shifts measure how the corrected masses depend on the analysis choice.
They are added in quadrature, giving 0.19, 0.25 and 0.71\,\MeVcc{}.
The largest single shift, $-0.59\,\MeVcc$ for the \Ups{3S} at the tighter cut, is comparable to its statistical error, so part of it may be a statistical fluctuation.

The momentum scale dominates the uncertainty.
\Cref{fig:momentum-scale} tests whether it varies with the kinematics by fitting the \Ups{1S} peak position in six bins of dimuon momentum and of soft muon \pT{}, in data and in simulation.
The simulation has a perfect momentum scale, so the difference between the two separates the detector effect from the shape bias.
In both variables the difference sits near $-4.5\times10^{-4}$, a common offset in the scale.
Against soft muon \pT{} it is flat ($\chi^2/\mathrm{ndof} = 5.9/5$).
Against momentum it varies more than the statistical errors allow ($66.9/5$), but its spread of $2.6\times10^{-4}$ stays within the $3\times10^{-4}$ calibration uncertainty of LHCb~\cite{LHCb:2023ood}.
We therefore assign the full $3\times10^{-4}$ to each mass, or about 3\,\MeVcc{}.

\Cref{tab:final} gives the final masses.
All three lie below their PDG values, by 4.45, 4.96 and 5.24\,\MeVcc{}, and the offsets grow with mass.
A single scale factor fitted to the three peaks describes them well, with $s - 1 = (-4.75 \pm 0.14)\times10^{-4}$ and $\chi^2/\mathrm{ndof} = 1.44/2$.
Calibrating the scale on the \Ups{1S} instead brings the \Ups{2S} and \Ups{3S} masses to within one standard deviation of their PDG values.
The offsets are therefore one momentum-scale error, not a fault in the peak model.
At $-4.75\times10^{-4}$ it is larger than the uncertainty LHCb quotes for its calibrated scale, which suggests these data were not processed with the final momentum calibration.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% SCOPE: the three masses and what limits them (momentum scale); what the fit studies showed
%%        (MC tails rejected, degeneracies confined to shape parameters, kinematic bins reduce the
%%        shape dependence); what would be done next (sigma parameterised in pT and y; a curved
%%        background per cell; a momentum-scale calibration of this sample). Half a page.

We measured the masses of the \Ups{1S}, \Ups{2S} and \Ups{3S} mesons with extended binned maximum-likelihood fits to 1.9 million selected dimuon candidates from the LHCb 2015 data.
Starting from a single Gaussian, we extended the signal model to a double Crystal Ball, which describes both the radiative tail and the spread of detector resolutions, and fitted it simultaneously in 12 cells of rapidity and transverse momentum (\cref{sec:r-gauss,sec:r-shape,sec:r-selection}).
A cut on the soft muon \pT{} at 3\,\GeVc{} raised the \Ups{1S} purity from 0.59 to 0.78 while keeping the statistical errors near their minimum.
The masses were corrected for the shape bias measured on simulation, and pseudo-experiments confirmed that the fit returns unbiased masses with correctly estimated errors.

The final masses are
\begin{align*}
    m_{1S} &= 9455.95 \pm 0.13 \pm 0.19 \pm 2.84\,\MeVcc, \\
    m_{2S} &= 10018.44 \pm 0.27 \pm 0.25 \pm 3.01\,\MeVcc, \\
    m_{3S} &= 10349.86 \pm 0.44 \pm 0.71 \pm 3.10\,\MeVcc,
\end{align*}
where the uncertainties are statistical, fit model and momentum scale (\cref{tab:final}).
The momentum scale is several times larger than the other two combined.
All three masses lie below their PDG values~\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2024cfk}, by 4.45, 4.96 and 5.24\,\MeVcc{}, about 1.6 times their total uncertainty.
The offsets grow in proportion to the mass and are described by a single scale factor of $(-4.75 \pm 0.14)\times10^{-4}$.
Calibrating on the \Ups{1S} brings the other two masses within one standard deviation of their PDG values.
The discrepancy is therefore a momentum-scale offset in the data rather than a fault of the mass model, and it is larger than the $3\times10^{-4}$ uncertainty of LHCb's final calibration~\cite{LHCb:2023ood}.
With statistical and fit-model precision already below 1\,\MeVcc{}, a more precise measurement now depends on that calibration.
